Does Dictating Emails and Slack Messages Save Time?

21 October 2026 5 min read

Dictation saves time on long, explanatory messages, where typing means drafting and redrafting. It loses on short, precise replies, where the cycle of activating the hotkey, speaking, and reviewing costs more than typing "yep, merged" ever would. The real friction isn't transcription speed. It's the editing pass afterwards for tone and punctuation.

So the useful question isn't "is dictation faster than typing?" It's "which messages are worth dictating?" The answer depends on length, on how much thinking the message needs, and on how much the recipient will read into your tone.

Why the question matters

Most people speak faster than they type, and for a long message that gap is real. But raw words per minute is the wrong measure, because most of the time spent on a long email isn't keystrokes. It's the stop-start loop of writing a sentence, deleting it, rephrasing it, and losing the thread.

Typing also taxes your hands. If you write a lot of long-form messages, such as incident summaries, design feedback, or replies to a customer who needs the full story, the cumulative strain is a legitimate reason to shift some of that load to your voice.

The failure mode runs the other way too. Dictation adds fixed overhead to every message: trigger it, wait for it to start listening, speak, wait for the transcript, then read it back. That overhead is trivial against 300 words and ruinous against six. A tool that makes a three-word Slack reply take longer than typing it hasn't saved you anything. It has moved the friction somewhere less visible.

Where dictation wins and where it loses

Dictation wins when the message is long, explanatory, and mostly about something you already understand. Think of a status update, a "here's why I'd avoid that approach" reply, or a first draft of documentation. Talking through it forces a linear structure, and you stop second-guessing every clause because the words are already out.

It loses on short, precise replies: a one-line approval, a ticket number, a command someone should run. These are mostly symbols and exact strings. Spoken, they need correcting almost every time, and typing them is already near-instant.

Messages where tone carries weight sit in the middle. Telling a colleague their pull request needs rework is something you can dictate, but you'll want to reread it carefully. Spoken phrasing tends to be blunter or more rambling than written phrasing, and the transcript won't soften it for you.

The actual friction: the editing pass

Whisper-family models produce punctuation and capitalisation, but they're guessing from how you spoke. Commas land where you paused, not necessarily where the grammar wants them. Sentence boundaries drift in long run-on thoughts. Proper nouns, library names, and acronyms are hit and miss.

That makes a review pass non-negotiable for anything you'd be embarrassed to send raw. The honest accounting is: dictation time, plus a skim-and-fix pass, versus typing time plus the redrafting you'd have done anyway. For a long message the first usually wins. For a short one it almost never does.

Edge cases and honest limitations

Whisper models have known weak spots. Strong accents, fast speech, and background noise all raise the error rate, and technical vocabulary (package names, internal service names, anything uncommon) is a frequent source of corrections. If you're in an open-plan office, the noise problem alone may erase the gains.

Dictating into a workspace chat also means thinking aloud near other people. That's awkward for sensitive content, and it's worth separating from the question of whether the tool records anyone. If your use case is capturing spoken content from meetings, the considerations are different, and we go through them in taking meeting notes by voice without recording everyone.

On the Linux side, text injection on Wayland depends on ydotool, so you'll need it installed and its daemon running. A tool that runs as a background service is also only useful if it stays up, which is what running a background tool with systemd --user covers. Wake-word activation is experimental, and a hotkey is the more reliable trigger for now.

One thing to do today

Pick the next message you'd normally spend five minutes drafting, such as a long email reply or a detailed Slack explanation, and dictate it instead. Time the whole thing, including the edit pass. Then do the same for three short replies. Comparing those two numbers will tell you more about whether dictation fits your work than any general claim, including ours.
